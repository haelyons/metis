# Can molecular mapping of AAV capsid–receptor interactions on brain microvascular endothelial cells determine transcytosis vs. intracellular retention?

## 1. Current understanding (relayed before answering)

The question sits at the intersection of structural virology, blood–brain barrier (BBB) cell biology, and capsid engineering. My working model before diving in is the following.

**The barrier and the two fates.** The BBB is built from brain microvascular endothelial cells (BMECs) joined by tight junctions, which restrict entry of most macromolecules and viruses from blood into brain parenchyma (https://www.cell.com/cell-reports/fulltext/S2211-1247(25)01190-8). A systemically administered AAV particle that binds a BMEC can end up in one of (at least) two distinct fates: (i) **transcytosis** — vectorial transport across the endothelial monolayer and release on the abluminal side without productive transduction of the endothelium itself; or (ii) **intracellular retention** — internalization followed by endosomal/lysosomal trapping or productive transduction of the endothelium, with the particle never reaching the brain parenchyma. These two fates are experimentally separable: in primary human BMEC transwell models, AAV9 crosses the monolayer by active transcytosis with little endothelial transduction, whereas AAV2 is endocytosed and transduces the BMECs but does not cross (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5298820/; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6879147/).

**The receptor landscape.** AAV entry is classically parsed into glycan attachment factors plus a proteinaceous entry receptor. The universal entry receptor AAVR (KIAA0319L) is essential for most serotypes and binds directly to capsids (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26814968/). Distinct serotypes engage distinct PKD domains of AAVR: AAV2 uses PKD2, AAV5 uses PKD1, and AAV1/8 require a combination of PKD1+2 (https://journals.asm.org/doi/10.1128/jvi.00391-17). AAV2's attachment receptor is heparan sulfate proteoglycan (HSPG) (https://www.cell.com/trends/molecular-medicine/fulltext/S1471-4914(20)30257-4). Separately, a set of *BBB-transcytosis* receptors has been identified for engineered capsids: LY6A for AAV-PHP.B/PHP.eB in mice (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30819613/; https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0225206), LRP6 for lab-evolved brain-tropic capsids (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11381518/), human TfR1 for BI-hTFR1 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38753766/), and human carbonic anhydrase IV (CA-IV) (https://www.cell.com/cell-reports/fulltext/S2211-1247(25)01190-8). A near-universal finding is that most mouse BBB-crossing capsids selected by directed evolution converge on LY6A or LY6C1, which are absent in primates (https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/article?id=10.1371/journal.pbio.3002112).

**Molecular mapping already exists.** Cryo-EM structures have resolved capsid–receptor interfaces: AAV2–AAVR PKD2 at 2.8 Å, with PKD2 binding the spike adjacent to the 3-fold axis (https://www.rcsb.org/structure/6IHB); AAV-PHP.eB at 2.24 Å showing the engineered 7-mer loop adopting a bent Lys–Asp-stabilized conformation that adds a LY6A interaction while partially destabilizing AAVR-PKD2 binding (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36034770/); and AAV9/PHP.eB–AAVR complexes showing PKD2 at the 3-fold protrusions and 2/5-fold wall (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35755945/). So "molecular mapping" is not hypothetical — it is an active, structure-resolved enterprise.

## 2. Predictions (made before answering)

1. **Binding signature will be necessary but not sufficient.** I predict the mapping from capsid–receptor binding signature to transcytosis-vs-retention is *not* a deterministic one-to-one function. There should be cases where two capsids engage the *same* receptor yet have different fates.
2. **The identity of the engaged receptor (and epitope) will be the dominant variable.** Engaging a constitutively recycling / receptor-mediated-transcytosis (RMT) receptor on the luminal BMEC surface (LY6A, TfR1, CA-IV, LRP6, LDLR) should predict transcytosis; engaging attachment/entry receptors that route toward lysosomes or the trans-Golgi network (HSPG; AAVR in certain contexts) should predict intracellular retention.
3. **Binding valence and epitope topology will modulate fate.** Monovalent, apical-domain engagement of a transcytosis receptor should favor transport; strong, multivalent, or "sticky" glycan binding should favor entrapment.
4. **Therefore molecular mapping will be *predictive within a receptor class* but not fully *deterministic*.** Post-binding intracellular sorting (recycling vs. degradation vs. TGN routing) will act as an independent, context-dependent determinant that binding data alone cannot fully resolve.

## 3. Proceeding: what the evidence shows

### 3.1 Binding identity does discriminate the two fates

The cleanest demonstration that binding *which receptor* matters is the AAV9-vs-AAV2 contrast in human BMECs: AAV9 transcytoses without transducing the endothelium, AAV2 is endocytosed and transduces the endothelium without crossing (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5298820/). AAV9 uses terminal N-linked galactose as a primary receptor, while AAV2 uses HSPG (https://www.cell.com/trends/molecular-medicine/fulltext/S1471-4914(20)30257-4). Engineered capsids make the point even more sharply: AAV-PHP.eB transcytosis in C57BL/6J mice is abolished in *Ly6a* knockouts, showing the receptor interaction is *required* for transport (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30819613/). Reciprocally, ectopic LY6A expression is sufficient to confer enhanced PHP.eB binding and transduction on HEK293T and CHO cells, and this LY6A mode operates independently of AAVR and galactose (https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0225206). Receptor-informed engineering then produced the strongest causal tests: BI-hTFR1, selected purely for binding human TfR1, is actively transported across human brain endothelial monolayers and gives 40–50× CNS expression in human *TFRC* knock-in mice but not wild-type mice (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38753766/), and CA-IV-targeting capsids achieve ~100-fold brain transduction over AAV9 in "humanized" mice (https://www.cell.com/cell-reports/fulltext/S2211-1247(25)01190-8). LRP6 was likewise identified by cell-surface interactome screens as a transcytosis receptor for engineered brain-tropic capsids (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11381518/). So there is a real, exploitable subset of "transcytosis binding signatures": those that engage a bona fide RMT receptor on the luminal BMEC surface.

### 3.2 But binding is not sufficient — the decisive counterexamples

Three lines of evidence falsify the strong hypothesis "binding signature → transcytosis":

- **Same receptor, different capsid context, different fate.** Transferring the PHP.B 7-mer peptide onto AAV1 (AAV1-PHP.B) does *not* confer Ly6A binding; adding the peptide plus the AAV9 HVR-VIII residues *does* confer Ly6A binding and brain localization, yet this AAV1-based vector still **does not penetrate the BBB**, unlike AAV9-PHP.B (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34346767/). Ly6A binding is therefore necessary but not sufficient; the AAV9 capsid context is required for the transport step.
- **Strong glycan binding routes capsids into dead-end intracellular uptake.** Capsids with high HSPG affinity (AAV2, AAV3b, AAV6) are especially prone to AAVR-independent, non-productive "dead entry" — cellular entry without transgene expression — because AAV2's productive route runs through AAVR to the trans-Golgi network rather than direct endosomal escape (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13483459/). Consistently, when transcytosis is blocked, transduction efficiency of brain-endothelial capsids actually *increases*, underscoring that transcytosis and intracellular (productive) retention are competing branches downstream of binding (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10975205/).
- **Binding valence/epitope, not just identity, governs sorting.** In the antibody-shuttle literature, monovalent engagement of the transferrin receptor out-performs bivalent engagement for transcytosis, because bivalent cross-linking diverts cargo away from the normal transport route (https://www.cell.com/neuron/fulltext/S0896-6273(13)01035-0). By analogy, how a capsid engages its receptor (stoichiometry, domain, avidity) is a variable independent of the mere presence of a binding signature.

### 3.3 The structural signature-level detail

The cryo-EM work shows that the *same* engineered insert can have opposite effects on the two relevant receptors. In PHP.eB, the 7-mer loop's Lys–Asp-bent conformation simultaneously **creates the LY6A binding platform and destabilizes AAVR-PKD2 binding** (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36034770/). The inserted loop length (not sequence) alone modulates PKD2 affinity via steric clash (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36034770/). Thus a single capsid surface region encodes a *vector* of receptor interactions (gain of one receptor, loss of another), and the net fate — transcytosis vs. retention — is a readout of that combined signature, not of any single binary binding event. The AAV1-PHP.B vs. AAV9-PHP.B comparison then shows the readout also depends on the capsid background beyond the interface (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34346767/).

### 3.4 What this means for "determine"

Molecular mapping can **determine**, with high confidence, *which class* a capsid falls into for the known receptors: signatures engaging LY6A/TfR1/CA-IV/LRP6/LDLR-class RMT receptors are the transcytosis signatures; signatures engaging HSPG (and AAVR-PKD2-dependent entry in a non-transport context) are the intracellular-retention signatures. But molecular mapping **cannot by itself determine** the transcytosis fate of an arbitrary novel capsid, because (a) binding is necessary but not sufficient (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34346767/), (b) the same binding signature has different outcomes depending on capsid background and valence (https://www.cell.com/neuron/fulltext/S0896-6273(13)01035-0), and (c) the post-binding sorting machinery (recycling vs. lysosomal vs. TGN routing) is a separate, context-dependent layer that binding footprints do not capture (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13483459/). The correct summary is: **binding signature is necessary and highly predictive *within a receptor class*, but the transcytosis-vs-intracellular decision is not reducible to the binding interface alone.** Transcytosis is a property of the receptor-plus-capsid-context-plus-trafficking system, and AAV transcytosis is serotype- and cell-type specific (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1525001605016989).

## 4. Role of computation

Machine learning and structural computation are genuinely useful for the *"for which binding signatures"* half of the question. Deep neural networks trained on deep-mutational data predict capsid viability across hundreds of thousands of AAV2 variants (201,426 variants → 110,689 viable) (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33574611/), and multi-trait pipelines (Fit4Function) combine binding, production, and biodistribution readouts to propose *de novo* capsids (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12816303/). In principle, parallel docking + ML could enumerate candidate capsid–receptor binding signatures at genome-scale. However, none of this computation changes the *determinability* answer derived above: the necessary-but-not-sufficient nature of binding is an experimentally established fact (AAV1-PHP.B, HSPG dead-entry), not a gap that more floating-point operations can close. The binding→fate map is limited by the scarcity of experimentally measured transcytosis labels and by genuine, context-dependent biology, not by compute throughput.

## 5. Verdict reasoning (compute-boundness)

The question "can molecular mapping *determine* which variants transcytose vs. remain intracellular, and for which binding signatures" is answerable now, with nuance, from published experiments (cryo-EM structures, transwell assays, Ly6a KO / TFRC KI / CA-IV humanized mice). The answer — *partially: yes for signatures engaging RMT-class receptors, no as a general deterministic rule because binding is necessary but not sufficient* — is grounded in existing data and would not be altered by additional parallel computation. The residual uncertainty is empirical (more receptors, more capsid contexts, more species), not computational. Therefore the problem is not compute-bound.

VERDICT: NOT COMPUTE-BOUND
