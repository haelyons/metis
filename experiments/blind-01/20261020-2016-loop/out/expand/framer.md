# Framing: Can molecular mapping of AAV capsid–receptor interactions on brain microvascular endothelial cells determine transcytosis vs. intracellular fate?

## 1. What the question is actually asking (operational definitions)

Before answering, I will define the terms so the question has a sharp, testable meaning.

- **Molecular mapping** = for each AAV capsid variant, enumerating its interactions with the surface of brain microvascular endothelial cells (BMECs): (a) receptor / attachment-factor **identity** (AAVR/KIAA0319L, transferrin receptor TfR1, LY6A/LY6C1, CA4, ALPL, terminal galactose, heparan sulfate proteoglycan/HSPG, integrins, etc.); (b) binding **affinity** (KD) and on/off kinetics; (c) **avidity/valency** (a 60-fold symmetric capsid can engage many receptor copies at once, producing apparent affinities far below the monovalent KD); (d) **epitope** (which receptor domain or loop the capsid contacts — e.g., the TfR1 apical domain vs. the transferrin-binding site); and (e) **pH-dependence** of binding (release in acidified endosomes).
- **Transcytose** = apical (luminal) → basolateral (abluminal) transport across the BMEC with release into the brain parenchyma, *without* the capsid being retained in — or transducing — the endothelial cell itself.
- **Remain intracellular** = the capsid is retained in the BMEC, either (i) transducing the endothelial cell (nuclear delivery, as AAV2 does) or (ii) being trapped/degraded in endolysosomal compartments and never released abluminally.

So the question has two parts: (1) can the binding signature *determine* which of these two fates a given variant takes, and (2) can it specify *which* binding signatures map to each fate (i.e., a signature→fate classifier).

## 2. Current understanding (relaying what is already established)

**The BBB as the relevant substrate.** The blood–brain barrier is formed by BMECs joined by tight junctions with suppressed paracellular and vesicular transport, so any macromolecule that reaches the brain must use an active transcellular route, principally receptor-mediated transcytosis (RMT) or a related vesicular program (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00018-018-2982-x). RMT is a four-step process — receptor binding, endocytosis, intracellular sorting/trafficking, and abluminal exocytosis — and the fate decision (transcytosis vs. lysosomal degradation vs. retention) is made at the *sorting* step, not at the *binding* step (https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-pharmtox-010814-124852).

**AAV capsid architecture and the site of receptor binding.** AAV is a ~25 nm, T=1 icosahedral particle built from 60 copies of VP1/VP2/VP3 in roughly a 1:1:10 ratio (https://www.mdpi.com/1999-4915/13/1/101). The common VP3 core is an eight-stranded β-barrel whose inter-strand loops form nine variable regions (VR-I…VR-IX) that constitute the surface topology and the receptor/antibody footprints (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9479082/). Receptor-binding residues cluster at the three-fold protrusions and the 2/5-fold wall (https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.162250899; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6537878/). Because a capsid presents 60 copies of its surface, receptor engagement is inherently **multivalent/avid**, and engineered variants typically differ only in a short peptide (e.g., a 7-mer inserted after VP1 residue 588 in AAV9) (https://www.nature.com/articles/nbt.3440).

**The two canonical fates, side by side, in the same cell type.** In primary human BMEC cultures, AAV2 and AAV9 partition oppositely: AAV2 transduces BMECs and remains intracellular with perinuclear/nuclear accumulation, while AAV9 is transported across the monolayer. AAV9's crossing is active (89.7% reduction at 4 °C) whereas AAV2's is temperature-independent/paracellular-leak associated (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5298820/; https://doi.org/10.1111/jnc.13861). So the same cell type exhibits both fates depending on the capsid.

**The receptor identity encodes a trafficking program.** The variants that robustly cross the BBB bind receptors whose endogenous trafficking is transcytotic or raft-associated:
- AAV9 was the first serotype shown to cross the BBB after intravenous delivery in mice, transducing CNS neurons/astrocytes (https://www.nature.com/articles/nbt.1515).
- AAV-PHP.B and AAV-PHP.eB, selected by Cre-recombination-based targeted evolution (CREATE), cross the BBB ~40-fold (or more) better than AAV9 in C57BL/6J mice (https://www.nature.com/articles/nbt.3440), and this phenotype was mapped, by whole-exome linkage and knockout/blocking experiments, to direct binding of the capsid to the GPI-anchored protein **LY6A (Sca-1)** with sub-nanomolar *apparent* (avid) affinity (0.07 nM), whereas the non-crossing parent AAV9 does not detectably bind LY6A (https://www.cell.com/molecular-therapy-family/molecular-therapy/fulltext/S1525-0016(19)30054-1).
- LY6A is a mouse-specific gene: primates lack a homolog, which explains why AAV-PHP.B fails to cross in non-human primates and instead showed dose-limiting acute toxicity (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5911151/).
- BI-hTFR1 was engineered to bind the **apical domain of human transferrin receptor (TfR1)**, a canonical RMT receptor at the BBB; it is actively transported across human brain endothelial monolayers and gives 40–50× more CNS expression than AAV9 in human-TFRC knock-in mice, with no enhancement in wild-type mice (https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adm8386; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10769326/). TfR1 transcytoses iron-bound transferrin across brain capillary endothelium without lysosomal degradation (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8967351/).
- VCAP-102 crosses via the GPI-anchored protein **ALPL** (tissue-nonspecific alkaline phosphatase), with >100-fold transcytosis upon ectopic ALPL expression (https://www.cell.com/molecular-therapy-family/molecular-therapy/fulltext/S1525-0016(25)00373-9). Several other BBB-crossing capsids (PHP.B → LY6A, BI28 → LY6C1, AAV-F → Car4) all bind GPI-anchored proteins (https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adm8386).

**A universal receptor that does not, by itself, decide fate.** AAVR (KIAA0319L) is a type-I transmembrane protein that is essential for infection by all tested AAV serotypes and binds AAV2 with ~150 nM affinity; AAVR traffics to the trans-Golgi network (https://www.nature.com/articles/nature16465; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4962915/). Critically, both transcytosing (AAV9) and non-transcytosing (AAV2) capsids use AAVR — so AAVR binding alone cannot distinguish fates; the discriminating information lives in the *additional* attachment factors and their trafficking programs.

## 3. The mechanistic crux: binding is upstream of — and not identical to — the fate decision

Three observations show that the fate decision is **not** a pure function of "which receptor is bound":

1. **Binding identity alone does not determine outcome.** BR1 (an AAV2-derived variant) transduces BMECs with little transcytosis, yet a single Q→N substitution at position 587 (BR1N) confers strong BBB penetration while using the *same* receptor. The authors state this directly: "receptor binding alone does not determine the final outcome in vivo" (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10036519/).
2. **Valency/affinity flips the sorting decision on the same receptor.** For TfR1, bivalent or high-affinity engagement drives the antibody–receptor complex to lysosomes, while monovalent or lower-affinity engagement is sorted into transcytosis; receptor clustering that blocks entry into sorting tubules is the proposed mechanism (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00018-018-2982-x). This is exactly why a binding *signature* must include valency/affinity/epitope/pH-dependence, not just identity.
3. **Transcytosis and transduction can be anti-correlated on the same receptor.** Blocking AAV transcytosis (e.g., with tannic acid) *increases* transduction of barrier epithelia, indicating that the fraction of capsids that would have transcytosed instead becomes available for intracellular infection — i.e., transcytosis is partly a default that competes with intracellular retention (https://www.cell.com/molecular-therapy-family/molecular-therapy/fulltext/S1525-0016(05)01698-9).

## 4. Predictions

**P1 (fate is underdetermined by receptor identity alone).** Any mapping that reports only *which* receptors a capsid binds will fail to separate transcytosing from intracellular variants, because both AAV2 and AAV9 engage AAVR, and BR1/BR1N share a receptor yet take opposite fates. I predict a classifier using receptor identity alone achieves ~chance separation on the known variant set.

**P2 (an extended binding signature is largely — but not perfectly — sufficient).** If "molecular mapping" is extended to the composite signature {receptor identity, avidity/valency, affinity, epitope, pH-dependence}, the known variants separate cleanly: transcytosing variants engage GPI-anchored proteins (LY6A, LY6C1, Car4, ALPL) or an RMT receptor at a non-ligand-competitive epitope with moderate avidity (BI-hTFR1 on the TfR1 apical domain), while intracellular variants engage HSPG/AAVR-dominated routes with high-avidity or lysosome-routing engagement. I predict a learned classifier on this composite signature generalizes to held-out *known* variants but **fails out-of-domain** (new receptors, new species) because the mapping signature→fate is not derivable from binding physics alone.

**P3 (a single-residue flip cannot be predicted from static binding maps).** The BR1N Q587N result, and the TfR1 valency switch, imply that fate is set by downstream sorting (receptor clustering, tubule entry, GPI-anchored-protein-enriched/GEEC routing), which is not encoded in a static capsid–receptor interaction map. I predict docking/MD of the capsid–receptor interface alone will *not* recapitulate these flips without trafficking data.

**P4 (the literal answer is "partially yes, with a specific signature definition").** Molecular mapping can determine transcytosis vs. intracellular fate only if (i) the signature includes the *identity of the primary attachment factor/receptor* (because identity is a proxy for the trafficking program), plus (ii) quantitative valency/affinity/epitope features (because the same receptor can route either way), and (iii) the mapping is calibrated per-species (because LY6A does not exist in primates). It cannot determine fate from binding in the absence of receptor-trafficking knowledge, because binding is necessary but not sufficient.

## 5. Is this compute-bound?

**The determining step is empirical, not computational.** The causal link "binding signature → transcytosis vs. intracellular" is established by *measured* trafficking outcomes (transwell transport, in vivo biodistribution, sorting assays), not by solving equations. The decisive counterexamples — BR1N's single-residue flip, the TfR1 valency/affinity switch, AAV2 vs. AAV9 on the same universal receptor — are observations that no amount of docking compute would have produced a priori, because the fate decision is executed by the endothelial cell's sorting machinery (clustering, tubule entry, GEEC/caveolae routing, abluminal exocytosis), which is not a function of the binding interface.

**Where compute *does* help — and why it is not the binding constraint:**
1. *Scaling the mapping itself.* High-throughput structural prediction/docking (AlphaFold-style or Rosetta) of many variants against candidate BMEC receptors (AAVR, TfR1, LY6A/LY6C1, Car4, ALPL, galactose, HSPG) could enumerate binding signatures for thousands of sequences. This expands the (sequence→binding) map, but the map's downstream half (binding→fate) still requires experimental labels.
2. *Simulating sorting.* Molecular dynamics of capsid–receptor valency/clustering and endosomal sorting is far from able to predict tubule entry or GEEC routing at the fidelity and timescale needed; it is a research program, not a reliable determiner.
3. *Interpolative ML.* A model trained on (sequence + binding features → fate) could interpolate across variant space, but the label set is small (dozens of well-characterized variants), so the bottleneck is ground-truth fate data, not FLOPs.

**Conclusion.** The answer to the posed question is gated by biological data and mechanism (receptor-trafficking coupling, species/strain context, endothelial sorting), not by parallel computation. More compute would refine the *binding map* but would not change the *determination* of transcytosis vs. intracellular fate, which is an empirical property of the endothelial sorting program. The problem is data- and experiment-bound, not compute-bound.

VERDICT: NOT COMPUTE-BOUND
