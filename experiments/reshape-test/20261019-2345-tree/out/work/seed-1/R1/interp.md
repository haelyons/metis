# Are there mech-interp (Goodfire-style) solutions to the RR-calibration compute question?

## Short answer

Goodfire-style mechanistic interpretability is a specific tool: train a sparse autoencoder (SAE) on a neural network's internal activations to recover a sparse dictionary of interpretable "features," then steer those features to test causal hypotheses (Goodfire, https://www.goodfire.com/research/understanding-and-steering-llama-3). The method is motivated by superposition — the hypothesis that networks pack more features than neurons into an overcomplete set of non-orthogonal directions, which SAEs are designed to disentangle (Cunningham et al. 2023, https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.08600).

The framer's core question — whether long MCMC chains are limited by hardware or by posterior geometry — is not a question about a learned neural network. It is settled by the effective-sample-size definition of autocorrelation (Stan Reference Manual, https://mc-stan.org/docs/2_31/reference-manual/effective-sample-size.html) and by the sampler-theory diagnosis that slow mixing "is frequently caused by an inappropriate selection of the proposal distribution" (Vrugt et al. 2009, https://research.wur.nl/en/publications/accelerating-markov-chain-monte-carlo-simulation-by-differential-). A GR4J forward model and an MCMC proposal have no internal activations for an SAE to decompose and no feature for steering to move. So for the literal "does a shit ton of GPUs fix τ" question the honest answer is no — mech interp is the wrong tool.

But the framer's problem has two edges where Goodfire-style tools do apply: (a) the dominant modern rainfall-runoff model is now a learned neural network (an LSTM), and (b) the "geometry" behind long chains is exactly a "which directions actually matter" question, which is what features-plus-interventions are built to answer. Three concrete options follow.

## SAE feature extraction on learned rainfall-runoff models

LSTM rainfall-runoff models already exist and are known to have interpretable internals: Kratzert et al. found that individual LSTM memory cells track snow accumulation/melt and soil-moisture states, and that the cell states behave as storages (https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/22/6005/2018/; https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.07903). Goodfire's method turns this from hand-inspection into an automated sparse dictionary: an SAE trained on the cell-state activations recovers monosemantic feature directions, which is what Cunningham et al. showed for language models (https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.08600) and what King et al. have begun for geophysical MLPs, where SAEs reduced the average number of sensitive inputs per neuron by 33% and surfaced monosemantic features tied to temperature and fall-speed thresholds for precipitation phase (https://doi.org/10.1029/2025jh000769). The RR-specific payoff is to re-ask the framer's equifinality question at the feature level: which sparse features of the learned model are uniquely constrained by the runoff record, and which interchangeable feature sets reproduce it — a representation-level statement of Beven & Binley's equifinality (https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/hyp.3360060305).

## Feature steering to map the identifiable manifold

Goodfire's steering intervenes on a single SAE feature and observes the downstream change (https://www.goodfire.com/research/understanding-and-steering-llama-3); the SAE-V work generalizes this into controlled interventions that validate a feature's causal role (https://arxiv.org/html/2502.06755v1). Applied to a differentiable RR model — an LSTM, or GR4J with the automatic differentiation Krapu et al. used for Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/2021WR031377) — one could steer each feature and measure the change in simulated runoff or likelihood. The features that move the hydrograph are the directions the data identify; the features that do not are the equifinality directions that set τ. This is a causal map of the posterior geometry the framer identifies as the binding constraint (https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2003WR002378), produced by intervention rather than by assuming a proposal.

## Active-subspace reparameterization of the calibration

The mech-interp instinct — a few sparse directions carry all the information — has a non-neural cousin that already attacks MCMC directly: active subspaces identify the low-dimensional directions along which a forward model actually varies and separate "the low-dimensional subspace that is informed by the data" from "its orthogonal complement that is constrained by the prior" (Constantine, Kent & Bui-Thanh 2015, https://doi.org/10.1137/15m1042127). Running MCMC on the active variables while sampling the prior on the inactive ones is precisely the reparameterization the framer identifies as the real lever on long chains, and the subspace can be estimated from M gradient samples via finite differences at two forward evaluations each, regardless of input dimension (https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1506.04190). The open question for RR specifically is whether a single linear subspace survives the artificial multimodality that cheap fixed-step integrators inject into the posterior (Schoups et al. 2010, https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdfdirect/10.1029/2009WR008648).

## What mech interp cannot touch

Structural and input (rainfall) error — the modeling/likelihood changes Renard et al. describe as altering the posterior — and the data-availability limit are not feature-decomposition problems (https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2009WR008328). No SAE or steering intervention recovers parameters a record cannot identify.
